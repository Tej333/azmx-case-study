01 · Context

Vision 2030 puts founders at the centre of the economy

The national plan asks small businesses to carry far more of the economy. Some targets are already met; the ones closest to founders are about halfway.

What has already changed · the front door has been rebuilt

1 register

One unified commercial register nationwide since April 2025: no expiry, just an annual data confirmation

~4,800

Services on the Saudi Business Center, integrated with around 80 government entities

~47,000

Companies established in the first half of 2026 alone

The reframe

Registration is no longer the hard part. Becoming operational is.

The design question shifts from "how do we simplify setup?" to "how do we get a founder from CR to first legal sale with confidence?"

So

The ambition and the money exist, and the front door works. The question is what happens to a founder after it.

02 · Founders

Most founders are consumer-facing, mid-career, and starting partly out of need

National survey and registry data describe a founder who looks very different from the tech startups that dominate the stories we hear.

Gender

47% of commercial records are owned by women; 23.2% of Saudi women are starting or running a new business.

Age

Starting peaks at 35–44 (21.9%). Under-25s start almost as often (19.3%) but only 3.4% own an established business.

Motivation

Build great wealth87.3%
Earn a living — jobs are scarce85.2%
Make a difference64.6%

Nationality

Saudi nationals start at 22%, expatriates at 11% — though both intend to start at nearly the same rate.

Sector

89% of new founders serve consumers. Yet 13 of 19 Founders KSA stories feature tech, finance or advisory.

Region

35.2% of new records are in Riyadh; about 30% come from outside the three main regions.

So

Support designed around tech startups misses most founders — and fear of failure stops more than half of those who could start.

Expectation gap 1

They expect speed; the market runs on trust

"Coffee before term sheets" — and silence isn't rejection.

Expectation gap 2

They expect a system; they get many doors

Ministries, accelerators and subsidies — sequenced by the founder alone.

Expectation gap 3

They expect signals; signals mislead

"An LOI is a maybe with letterhead." Repeat paying customers are the proof.

So

Founders don't lack services. They lack a way through them.

The middle is crowded

Five of nine steps sit in set up. The Saudi Business Center connects ~4,800 services across about 80 entities and auto-registers ZATCA, GOSI and the Chamber.

The ends are unowned

Learning, funding and scaling have no bundle from either side — where personas A, B and C struggle most.

Paying decides who gets carried

So

The gap isn't a missing service. It's the journey between services.

Core insight

Government has bundled the transactions. No one has bundled the journey — the sequence, the judgement, and the steps before and after setup.

Problem statement: Founders in Saudi Arabia move through a rich but fragmented system with no one owning the sequence. Those who can pay a bundler get carried; everyone else works it out alone — and many never start.

07 · Vision & north star

Vision

Every founder in the Kingdom — whoever they are, wherever they start — has one clear path from idea to a lasting business.

Starting a business becomes a guided journey, not a maze: the sequence is visible, the first step is cheap, and help comes from people as well as portals.

North star

More founders reach first revenue, sooner — without paying to find the way.

North-star metric

Share of new founders who issue their first invoice within 6 months of registering — reported for those who did and didn't use a paid bundler, and by persona.

For founders

Know the next step, try at low risk, and get carried through every stage — free.

For government

Existing programmes reach the right founders, and progress is measured on outcomes.

For the ecosystem

Private partners plug into one path and compete on speed and quality, not on access.

12 · Governance & operating model

The SBC orchestrates; a journey board owns the outcome

Entities keep their services and mandates. One cross-entity board holds the end-to-end measure. Ownership assignments are a proposal for discussion [A].

13 · Open questions

What we still need to learn

Research questions to validate first

  • Where do founders first learn what is required, and how far ahead do they plan?
  • Which permit surprises founders most, and in what order do they apply?
  • How do founders know they are compliant, and how many are fined in year one?
  • Does a second location repeat the same friction?

Risks and tensions

  • Government employees can't hold a CR — does "test before you quit" need the freelance route?
  • No public regional data on start-up rates or funding (persona C)
  • Most targets need a baseline before they can be set
  • Journey board mandate and entity roles are proposed, not agreed

What I would validate first

Week one would test the riskiest assumptions

Founders: 8–10 interviews with founders 0–12 months in, mapping where they actually stalled

Intermediaries: PRO agents and accountants, who see the failure patterns at scale

Entity teams: What requirement and status data already exists and can be shared

Service data: Drop-off and time between CR and first licence, by activity

Don't build a new door. Light the path behind the one that already works.
